Differential Privacy Demystified: Epsilon Budgets in Practice

Sai Pavan Aditya Mantripragada · February 2025 · 6 min read
Differential Privacy GDPR ML Opacus

Epsilon (ε) is the privacy budget in Differential Privacy — and it is almost universally misunderstood, misused, and miscommunicated. Teams choose ε = 1 because a paper used it. Regulators ask "what is your epsilon?" without knowing what answer is meaningful. Engineers tune ε to improve model accuracy without realising they have voided their privacy guarantee.

This post is a practical guide to thinking about ε correctly, composing privacy guarantees across multiple queries, and avoiding the silent failures that can expose sensitive data even in "private" systems.

What Epsilon Actually Means

A mechanism M satisfies (ε, δ)-Differential Privacy if, for any two adjacent datasets D and D′ (differing by one record), and any output S: 

Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε · Pr[M(D') ∈ S] + δ

Intuition: ε bounds the multiplicative advantage an adversary gains from your data being in vs. out of the dataset. Small ε (≈ 0.1) means strong privacy, high noise, lower utility. Large ε (≈ 10) means weak privacy, low noise, higher utility.

There is no universal "good" epsilon. ε = 1 for a medical record is very different from ε = 1 for a search query. Context always matters.

Rough practical guidance, borrowed from Apple and Google's deployments:

  • ε ≤ 1 — strong protection; suitable for highly sensitive data (medical, financial)
  • 1 < ε ≤ 10 — moderate; acceptable for many enterprise use cases with appropriate δ
  • ε > 10 — weak; questionable privacy; treat as "privacy-flavoured" rather than privacy-preserving

Budget Composition: Where Teams Go Wrong

Every query to a private dataset consumes budget. Run 100 training epochs, and you've composed 100 mechanisms. The key insight: you cannot reuse epsilon. The budget is spent.

Three composition theorems, in order of tightness:

  • Basic composition: k mechanisms with ε each → total ε_total = k·ε. Conservative and easy, but wasteful for large k.
  • Advanced composition: achieves ε_total ≈ √(2k ln(1/δ)) · ε for k mechanisms. Much tighter for many queries.
  • Rényi DP (RDP) accountant: tightest in practice; used by Opacus. Tracks moments of the privacy loss random variable for near-optimal composition.
# Using Opacus RDP accountant (PyTorch)
from opacus.accountants import RDPAccountant
from opacus import PrivacyEngine

privacy_engine = PrivacyEngine()
model, optimizer, data_loader = privacy_engine.make_private_with_epsilon(
    module=model,
    optimizer=optimizer,
    data_loader=train_loader,
    epochs=50,
    target_epsilon=3.0,   # target ε
    target_delta=1e-5,    # target δ
    max_grad_norm=1.0,    # gradient clipping
)

# After training, check actual spent budget
epsilon = privacy_engine.get_epsilon(delta=1e-5)
print(f"ε = {epsilon:.2f}, δ = 1e-5")

The δ Parameter Is Not a Free Lunch

(ε, δ)-DP has a failure probability δ: with probability at most δ, the guarantee breaks down entirely. The standard recommendation is δ < 1/n where n is the dataset size. For a dataset of 100,000 records, δ = 1e-6 is acceptable; δ = 0.01 is not.

I have seen teams use δ = 0.1 to get a "better" ε number for a slide deck. Do not do this. A large δ means your guarantee can fail with non-negligible probability — which is not a privacy guarantee at all.

Calibrating the Noise Multiplier

DP-SGD adds Gaussian noise σ · C to clipped gradients, where C is the gradient clipping norm. The noise multiplier σ controls the ε/accuracy trade-off. Higher σ → more privacy, lower accuracy. 

# Quick sensitivity analysis: how does σ affect ε?
from opacus.accountants.utils import get_noise_multiplier

sigma = get_noise_multiplier(
    target_epsilon=3.0,
    target_delta=1e-5,
    sample_rate=256 / 50000,  # batch_size / dataset_size
    epochs=50,
)
print(f"Required noise multiplier σ = {sigma:.3f}")

For most image classification tasks, σ between 0.8 and 1.5 gives acceptable utility at ε ≤ 3. For tabular data with high-dimensional features, you may need σ > 2 to avoid gradient leakage.

Common Pitfalls

  • Forgetting hyperparameter tuning leaks privacy. If you tune ε, learning rate, and architecture on the private dataset, each tuning step consumes budget. Use public proxies or reserve a separate budget for tuning.
  • Model output can leak training data. DP on gradients is not the same as DP on the model's predictions. Membership inference attacks can still succeed against DP models if ε is large enough. Audit your models.
  • Normalising input features without DP. Computing mean and std for feature normalisation on the private dataset uses information without accounting for it. Use DP statistics or public normalisers.
  • Ignoring post-processing. DP is post-processing invariant — any deterministic function of a DP output is also DP. You cannot make a model "less private" by additional processing. Use this to your advantage: apply transformations after the private step.

Summary

Differential Privacy gives you a rigorous, quantifiable guarantee. But only if you:

  • Choose ε and δ appropriate to your data sensitivity and dataset size
  • Track budget across all queries using the RDP accountant, not basic composition
  • Set a hard budget cap and terminate training when exhausted
  • Treat hyperparameter search, feature engineering, and evaluation as budget consumers
